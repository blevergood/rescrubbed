# Results: encrypted 30-day readmission risk scoring

_Generated by the Niobium FHE Application Design AI Assistant (FHEanna)._

This is a demonstration of encrypted computation, not a clinical tool. It is not
intended for real medical use.

**Verdict: PASS.** The encrypted service put all 17,498 held-out patients in the
same risk band as the unencrypted model, in every one of four run modes including
deployment on Niobium Fog hardware.

---

## How the design was validated

Written for a reader who is not a cryptographer.

### a. There is an answer key, made independently of the encryption work

Before any encrypted code existed, we built the ordinary version of this system:
a readmission risk model trained on 101,766 real de-identified hospital
encounters, run in plain arithmetic on a computer that could see everything. It
was measured against what happened to those patients, whether they came back
within 30 days. That is the answer key, and 17,498 patients were held out of
training so it could be checked on people it had never seen.

We then built a second plaintext version, the "twin", which does the encrypted
computation's exact arithmetic, including the polynomial stand-in described
below, in the open where we can inspect it. The twin reproduced the answer key's
bands for all 17,498 patients before a line of encrypted code was written.

### b. The test: same patients, encrypted, computed blind, compared

The 17,498 patients were encrypted, sent to a process holding no decryption key,
scored, and returned. Only then were they decrypted and compared.

| | Result |
|---|---|
| Patients whose band changed vs the twin | **0 of 17,498** |
| Largest numeric difference | **3.9 × 10⁻⁷** |
| Budget it had to stay under | 1 × 10⁻⁴ |
| Patients banded differently from the unencrypted model, end to end | **0 of 17,498** |

The measured difference is about 250× smaller than the budget. That budget was
fixed before the encrypted build, by measuring how close each patient sits to a
band boundary and how much drift the closest one could absorb.

### c. Where the difference comes from

Two sources, both quantified in advance.

**A polynomial stands in for a sharp cutoff.** Encrypted arithmetic can add and
multiply. Asking "is this score above the threshold?" is a comparison, which is
neither. The cutoff is therefore imitated by a smooth curve built from 191
multiplications and additions. Across the entire range of scores the model can
produce, that curve departs from the true cutoff shape by at most **0.000157**.

**Encryption carries noise.** The scheme used here trades exactness for speed:
every encrypted number carries a small perturbation. Measured here, it moved the
answer by at most **0.00000039**.

A patient's band is decided by rounding to the nearest of three values, so an
error would have to reach **0.5** to change anyone's band.

### d. Agreement across independent checks

| Layer | What it checks | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Weighted tally vs scikit-learn | Our arithmetic against a widely-used library we did not write | identical to the last bit |
| Twin vs answer key | The design, before encryption | 0 of 17,498 differ |
| Slot-level simulation in numpy | The single-patient data shuffling, before any C++ | reproduces the tally to 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ |
| Local encrypted run | Real encryption, plain CPU | 0 of 17,498 differ |
| Simulator | Recorded computation replayed | 0 of 17,498 differ |
| Recorded trace vs local OpenFHE | That the recording captured the computation | **bit-identical** |
| Niobium Fog hardware | The deployed run | 0 of 17,498 differ |

Four independent executions, on different machinery, agree patient for patient.

### e. What this does not establish

Everything above concerns whether the encrypted system reproduces the model. It
says nothing about whether the model predicts well.

On this dataset the best published result separates readmitted from
non-readmitted patients with a ranking quality of about 0.667. Ours reaches
0.635. Roughly 15% of the patients we flag as high-risk are readmitted, against a
9.1% background rate.

What changed is that the hospital never handed over a patient record and the
vendor never handed over its model.

## The three versions, and where quality is lost

| Comparison | What it isolates | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Reference vs ground truth** | The model's clinical worth | AUC **0.6352**; ceiling on these features 0.6440; published best on this dataset ~0.667; majority-class baseline (predict "no return") 90.9% accuracy at 0 recall |
| **Twin vs reference** | Cost of the polynomial stand-in | **0 of 17,498** bands differ; high-band precision 0.1543 → 0.1543, recall 0.2495 → 0.2495 (both unchanged to 5 decimals) |
| **FHE vs twin** | Cost of encryption alone | **0 of 17,498** bands differ; max error 3.9 × 10⁻⁷ against a 1 × 10⁻⁴ budget |

### Clinical picture

| Band | Patients | Share | Actually readmitted within 30 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| low | 10,527 | 60.2% | 6.2% |
| moderate | 4,405 | 25.2% | 12.3% |
| high | 2,566 | 14.7% | **15.4%** |
| all | 17,498 | 100% | 9.1% |

The high band carries 1.70× the background readmission rate. Of everyone who was
readmitted, 25.0% were flagged.

The cohort is harder than the one most published work on this dataset uses:
patients who died or went to hospice are removed, since they cannot be
readmitted, and only one encounter per patient is kept, so no patient appears in
both training and test.

### Head to head against the LACE index

Published c-statistics for the established bedside scores come from different
populations with different outcome definitions. The comparable test is to compute
one on our exact held-out patients (`scripts/compare_lace.py`):

| Model | AUC on our 17,498 | Flagged high-risk | Of those, readmitted | Lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ours** (running encrypted) | **0.6352** | 2,566 (14.7%) | **15.4%** | **1.70×** |
| LACE index, computed here | 0.5614 | 1,482 (8.5%) | 13.1% | 1.44× |
| Random | 0.5000 | | | 1.00× |

Three qualifications on that comparison:

- **The LACE figure is a floor.** Its comorbidity component needs a Charlson
  index from a full problem list. This dataset records only three diagnosis
  codes, so the median Charlson computable here is 1, which for most patients is
  the single point for being diabetic. That understates one of LACE's four
  inputs.
- **LACE's published 0.68 is measured on a different task**: general medical and
  surgical inpatients, predicting readmission or death, against all-cause
  readmission among survivors here.
- **HOSPITAL (published 0.71) was not tested.** It requires haemoglobin and
  sodium at discharge, which this dataset does not contain.

The directly comparable benchmarks are the published results on this dataset:
logistic regression around 0.642 and gradient boosting around 0.667. Ours sits at
0.635, and at 98.6% of the 0.644 ceiling measured on our own features.

## Deployment profile

### Parameters

| | |
|---|---|
| Scheme | CKKS (approximate arithmetic on real numbers) |
| Ring dimension | 65,536, giving 32,768 patient slots per ciphertext |
| Security | 128-bit classical (HEStd_128_classic) |
| Multiplicative depth | 12 (circuit uses 10; 2 spare) |
| Scaling / first modulus | 45 / 60 bits |
| Band polynomial | Chebyshev degree 191, steepness 24, fitted over [-4.356, 1.481] |
| Predicted decode margin | +94 bits, confirmed by 4 clean runs |
| Rotation keys | 0 for batch mode, 5 for single-patient mode |

### Measured runs

| Mode | Shape | Patients | Peak server memory | Band changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CPU | batch | 17,498 | 1,433 MiB | **0** |
| CPU | per patient | 1 | 706 MiB | **0** |
| Simulator | batch | 17,498 | 2,110 MiB | **0** |
| Simulator (real maths) | batch | 17,498 | 2,121 MiB | **0** |
| **Niobium Fog** | batch | 17,498 | n/a (remote) | **0** |

### Boundary sizes, estimated at design time and then measured

| | Estimated | Measured |
|---|---|---|
| One ciphertext | 13 MiB | 13.0 MiB |
| Batch upload (28 fields) | 364 MiB | **364.1 MiB** |
| Single-patient upload | 13 MiB | **13.0 MiB** |
| Result returned | 3 MiB | 4.0 MiB |
| Setup keys, batch mode | ~53 MiB | 67.0 MiB |
| Setup keys, single-patient mode | ~253 MiB | 337.0 MiB |

The result ciphertext came back one level higher than predicted, at 4 MiB rather
than 3, and the key material is larger than the rule-of-thumb formula suggests.

### Reading the cost figures

Per patient, the batch mode uploads 21.3 KiB. At a full 32,768-patient batch that
falls to 11.4 KiB per patient, because the ciphertexts are a fixed size whether or
not every slot is used. The 17,498-patient run fills a little over half of them.

The single-patient mode uploads 13 MiB to score one person, because a ciphertext
costs what it costs regardless of how few slots carry data. That is inherent to
encrypted computation at this security level, and it is why the batch shape
exists.

## Coverage

No patient was rejected. Extreme values are clipped to the published clinical
range, which makes the score's range provable rather than estimated, so a single
unusual patient cannot spoil a batch.

In the full 17,498-patient run, 450 field values across 445 patients (2.5%)
saturated at a published bound, for instance a 20-day stay scored as the 14-day
maximum. These are continuous clinical counts, where a value held at the bound
still carries the ordering the model uses. No categorical field is clipped.

## Reproducing this

```bash
make data                        # rebuild inputs from the public dataset
make model                       # re-freeze the design
make build                       # compile
./run.sh "./run_test.sh --cpu"   # local validation
./run.sh "./run_test.sh --sim"   # simulator rehearsal
./run.sh "./run_test.sh"         # the Fog (needs an API key)
./demo/run_demo.sh               # two-process deployment
```

Raw per-run figures are written to `run_<mode>/summary.json` alongside the run's
other artifacts. The design evidence that is tracked lives in
`reports/sweep.json` (the parameter frontier) and
`reports/candidate_validation.json` (the shortlisted settings).
